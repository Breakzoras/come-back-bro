## Part 1. Verified findings

### A. Pre-date nerves: breathing

**A1. Cyclic sighing (Balban et al. 2023, Cell Reports Medicine).** Full text.
- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/ (DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)
- Design: remote randomized trial, 108 randomized (mindfulness 24; cyclic sighing 30; box breathing 21; cyclic hyperventilation 33). 5 minutes per day for 28 days. Recruited from a Stanford undergraduate class and word of mouth.
- Protocol (cyclic sighing): a slow inhale, then a second shorter inhale to fill the lungs fully, then a slow, complete exhale. Repeat for 5 minutes. Ideally in through the nose and out through the mouth.
- Results: breathwork raised daily positive affect more than mindfulness; cyclic sighing was the only single technique significantly better than mindfulness on positive affect and on lowering respiratory rate. **State anxiety fell in all groups with no difference between breathwork and mindfulness.** Heart rate variability, resting heart rate and sleep did not change.
- Caveats stated by the authors: exploratory feasibility study, registered retrospectively; adherence self-reported; small groups; 4 weeks, no follow-up. Comparator was an active one (mindfulness); there was no no-practice arm.
- Strength: **moderate for mood with daily practice; weak for a single breath right before a date.** The trial tested a 5-minute daily routine and gives no direct evidence on one sigh in the moment.

**A2. Breathwork meta-analysis (Fincham et al. 2023, Scientific Reports).** Abstract via PubMed API.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y
- 12 RCTs, 785 adults for stress: g = -0.35 (small to medium). Anxiety (k = 20) g = -0.32; depressive symptoms (k = 18) g = -0.40. Most studies at moderate risk of bias; the authors warn against hype.
- Strength: **moderate** (small effects, moderate bias).

**A3. Slow breathing and the heart (Laborde et al. 2022, Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews).** Abstract via PubMed API.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711
- 223 studies. Voluntary slow breathing raised vagally mediated HRV during the session, immediately after one session, and after multi-session programmes. Few adverse effects expected.
- Strength: **strong for a physiological effect during and right after one session.** This is a body marker; it says less about how calm the person feels.

**A4. Slow breathing review (Zaccaro et al. 2018, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience).** Abstract via PubMed API.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353
- 15 studies in healthy people, breathing under 10 breaths per minute: more relaxation, comfort and alertness; less arousal, anxiety and anger reported.
- Strength: **weak to moderate** (few, small, heterogeneous studies).

**A5. NHS breathing exercise for stress.** Official health body.
- URL: https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/self-help/guides-tools-and-activities/breathing-exercises-for-stress/
- Breathe in gently counting 1 to 5, breathe out gently counting 1 to 5, repeat for at least 5 minutes; most benefit with daily practice. This works out at roughly 6 breaths per minute.
- Strength: **moderate** (official consensus advice, no trial behind this exact protocol); a suitable default for a public skill.

**A6. Body symptoms can come from other causes (NHS panic disorder page).**
- URL: https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/panic-disorder/
- Panic symptoms listed: racing heartbeat, feeling faint, chest pain, shortness of breath, choking feeling, dizziness, nausea, trembling. The page notes most of these can also be symptoms of other conditions.
- Strength: **moderate** (official guidance). Supports adding a "get medical advice if this feels unlike your usual nerves" line.

### B. Reappraising nerves as excitement

**B1. Brooks 2014, J Exp Psychol: General ("Get excited").** Abstract via PubMed API; per-study details from the replication paper (B2).
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035325
- Karaoke, public speaking and math tasks. Saying "I am excited" (compared with "I am calm") raised felt excitement and improved performance in the original studies. Study 2 (public speaking) had N = 140 and found more excitement **with no drop in felt anxiety**.
- Strength: **moderate for "feel more excited"; weak for "perform better"** (see B2).

**B2. Direct replication of Brooks Study 2 (Poynter and Smith Pasqualini 2025, Psychology of Language and Communication).** Full text (PDF).
- URL: https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.58734/plc-2025-0011.pdf (DOI https://doi.org/10.58734/plc-2025-0011)
- N = 74 (excited 34, calm 40; observer ratings 71). Self-report results nearly identical to Brooks: more excitement, same anxiety. **No difference on any observer rating of speech performance**, and no difference on an indirect anxiety measure (emotional Stroop).
- Strength: small sample, so it weakens the performance claim without settling it.

**B3. Jamieson's arousal reappraisal line.** Abstracts via PubMed API.
- Jamieson, Nock and Mendes 2012: telling people their arousal is functional produced more efficient cardiovascular responses and less attention bias to negative information. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025719
- Jamieson et al. 2022, community college field RCT, N = 339: less evaluation anxiety, better exam scores, lower cortisol on test days. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000893
- Journault et al. 2025, RCT N = 308: reappraisal plus goal framing raised exam scores and lowered cortisol. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2025.107544
- Oveis et al. 2020, N = 266: one teammate reappraising improved stress physiology in both teammates. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000757
- Mixed or null: Hangen et al. 2019, N = 279: better cardiovascular profile, no overall performance effect, benefits only in men in exploratory analysis. https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2018.1530049 . Oswald et al. 2026, N = 121, preregistered: no general effect on prosocial behaviour; an effect only in people who believed the intervention worked. https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2026.2615347
- Strength: **moderate** for "your racing heart is fuel for the moment" changing how stress feels and how the body responds; outcomes vary by setting and person. Almost all tests used exams, speeches or lab tasks; none tested dates.

### C. Sleep

**C1. Early romantic love and sleep (Brand et al. 2007, J Adolescent Health).** Abstract via PubMed API.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2007.01.012
- 107 adolescents (mean age about 18): 60 newly and intensely in love, 47 controls. Those in love scored higher on a hypomania scale, slept fewer hours, and reported **better** subjective sleep quality, **less** daytime sleepiness and **better** concentration (7-night sleep log, self-report).
- Strength: **weak to moderate** (small, adolescent, self-report, cross-sectional). Key point: people in early love may feel fine on less sleep.

**C2. Sleep loss and emotion meta-analysis (Palmer et al. 2024, Psychological Bulletin).** Abstract via PubMed API.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000410
- Preregistered; 154 experimental studies, 5,717 participants. All forms of sleep loss lowered positive affect (SMD -0.27 to -1.14) and **raised anxiety symptoms (SMD 0.57 to 0.63)**.
- Strength: **strong**.

**C3. Sleep loss and next-day anxiety (Ben Simon et al. 2020, Nature Human Behaviour).** Abstract via PubMed API.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0754-8
- Sleep deprivation raised anxiety, linked to weaker medial prefrontal activity; even modest night-to-night reductions in sleep predicted higher next-day anxiety.
- Strength: **moderate**.

**C4. Review: sleep and the emotional brain (Goldstein and Walker 2014, Annual Review of Clinical Psychology).** Abstract via PubMed API.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-032813-153716
- Synthesis arguing for a causal link between sleep and next-day emotional regulation. Narrative review.
- Strength: **moderate** (review).

**C5. Bedtime to-do list (Scullin et al. 2018, J Exp Psychol: General).** Full text via PubMed Central (PMC5758411).
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000374
- 57 healthy adults aged 18 to 30, one night of polysomnography. Writing tomorrow's to-do list for 5 minutes led to faster sleep onset than writing a list of completed tasks (d = 0.63); more specific to-do lists went with faster sleep onset.
- Caveats from the authors: a single first night in a lab, a healthy young sample, a comparison group that wrote about completed tasks (no no-writing group), mechanism unknown.
- Strength: **moderate** (one well-controlled small study).

**C6. Scheduled worry time (Oxford Health NHS Foundation Trust).**
- URL: https://www.oxfordhealth.nhs.uk/camhs/self-care/sleep/anxiety-worry/worry-time
- Set aside 20 to 30 minutes a day as "worry time", outside bedtime; write down everything that is worrying you, then list possible solutions and plan the steps.
- Strength: **weak to moderate** (clinical self-help guidance, no trial cited on the page).

**C7. NHS sleep advice.**
- Insomnia: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insomnia/ . Go to bed only when sleepy; same wake time daily; relax for an hour before bed; no alcohol, tea or coffee within 6 hours of bed; no screens right before bed; **do not drive when you feel sleepy**; exercise during the day and keep it at least 4 hours from bedtime; see a GP if the trouble lasts months or makes coping hard.
- Every Mind Matters: https://www.nhs.uk/every-mind-matters/mental-health-issues/sleep/ . Alcohol makes you sleepy at first and worsens sleep later in the night; put the phone away before bed.
- Strength: **moderate** (official consensus advice).

**C8. Sleep tips alone are weak for chronic insomnia (AASM guideline, Edinger et al. 2021).** Abstract via PubMed API.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986
- Strong recommendation for CBT for insomnia; conditional recommendations for stimulus control, sleep restriction and relaxation; a conditional recommendation **against** sleep hygiene as a stand-alone treatment for chronic insomnia.
- Strength: **strong** (GRADE guideline). Implication: tips suit a few racing-mind nights; weeks of bad sleep call for a professional.

**C9. Sleep loss and mania in bipolar disorder.** Abstracts via PubMed API.
- Lewis et al. 2017, J Affective Disorders: 870 parous women with bipolar disorder; those who reported sleep loss triggering mania had about twice the odds of postpartum psychosis (OR 2.09). Cross-sectional, recall bias. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.08.054
- Umlauf and Shattell 2005, Issues in Mental Health Nursing (review): sleep loss is an important trigger for mania. https://doi.org/10.1080/01612840591008267
- Strength: **weak to moderate** for the general point that sleep loss is a recognised trigger for some people with bipolar disorder.

### D. Alcohol on dates

**D1. Drink-driving limits.**
- WHO SAFER: general drivers at most 0.05 g/dL; novice and commercial drivers at most 0.02 g/dL. https://www.who.int/initiatives/SAFER/drink-driving
- WHO road traffic fact sheet: crash risk starts at low BAC and rises significantly at 0.04 g/dL or above. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries
- European Commission: the limit should be 0.5 g/L or lower for the general population; the novice limit should be 0 or just above 0; 0.8 g/L remains in 3 of 25 member states. https://road-safety.transport.ec.europa.eu/eu-road-safety-policy/priorities/safe-road-use/alcohol_en
- ETSC table (undated, mentions a July 2025 change): general limit 0.0 in Czech Republic, Hungary, Romania and Slovakia; 0.2 in Estonia, Norway, Poland and Sweden; 0.4 in Lithuania; 0.5 in most others; UK 0.8 with Scotland at 0.5; many countries set 0.0 to 0.2 for novices. https://etsc.eu/issues/drink-driving/blood-alcohol-content-bac-drink-driving-limits-across-europe/
- Unverified, snippet only (excluded): the US 0.08 g/dL limit (NHTSA page returned 403); the Scottish natural experiment on lowering the limit.
- Strength: **strong** (official legal and policy sources). Conclusion: limits differ by country and by driver type, and some countries allow zero.

**D2. Alcohol, judgment and sexual consent.**
- Abbey et al. 2001, Alcohol Research and Health (full text via PMC4484576, PMID 11496965): about half of sexual assaults involve alcohol use by the perpetrator, the victim or both; alcohol narrows attention to the most obvious cues and raises misperception of friendliness as sexual interest; drinking women reported taking risks they normally avoid (for example a ride home with a man they barely knew). The authors stress that the evidence is partly correlational and that the perpetrator alone is responsible. Strength: **moderate** (older narrative review, mixed designs).
- NHS Devon Sexual Health service: alcohol and drugs make it harder to notice signals like body language or hesitation; a person too drunk or high to understand what is happening cannot give consent; in the UK, sex with someone too intoxicated to consent is sexual assault. https://www.devonsexualhealth.nhs.uk/your-services/alcohol-and-drug-support/ . Strength: official local NHS service guidance.
- NHS spiking page: if spiked and assaulted, go to a sexual assault referral centre, GP, A&E or sexual health clinic; if spiked without assault, call 111 for worrying symptoms and tell the police. https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/healthy-body/drink-spiking-and-date-rape-drugs/ . Strength: **moderate** (official guidance).

**D3. Alcohol and diabetes.**
- Diabetes UK: alcohol makes hypos more likely; the risk is higher with insulin and some tablets such as sulphonylureas; **the risk can last up to 24 hours** after drinking; drinking on an empty stomach or heavily raises it; hypos are often mistaken for being drunk; carry hypo treatment and medical ID, and make sure the people you are with know you have diabetes and how to help; speak to your healthcare team when unsure. https://www.diabetes.org.uk/living-with-diabetes/eating/what-to-drink-with-diabetes/alcohol-and-diabetes
- American Diabetes Association: low blood glucose is the biggest concern, especially with insulin and sulfonylureas; hypoglycemia can strike hours after the last drink; its symptoms overlap with being drunk; snack while drinking; talk to your doctor. https://diabetes.org/health-wellness/alcohol-and-diabetes
- Unverified, snippet only (excluded): an NHS "8 to 12 hours" risk window; a "snack before bed" tip from a local NHS service.
- Strength: **strong** (consistent official guidance from two national diabetes bodies). Neither page gives dose changes to the public; both send people to their own care team.

### E. Meeting someone new safely

- New Zealand Police: meet face to face in a public area; bring a friend or tell someone your plans; keep surname, address, workplace and phone number private until you trust the person; never send money to people met on a dating site; leave early if it feels wrong. https://www.police.govt.nz/node/45268
- Virginia Tech Police: make your own travel arrangements to and from a first date; meet in a well-lit public place; tell a good friend where you are going and who you are meeting; carry a phone and, if possible, step away to call a friend during the date. https://police.vt.edu/safety-security/safety-tips/online-dating.html
- US Federal Trade Commission, romance scams: warning signs are a person who cannot meet in person, asks for money (emergencies, travel, visas), gift cards, crypto investing or wire transfers; never send money or gifts to someone you have not met in person; talk to someone you trust; reverse-image-search the profile photo. https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams
- Unverified, snippet only (excluded): South Australia Police, Get Safe Online, Denver and Lancashire police pages.
- Strength: **moderate** (consistent official police and regulator advice across two countries, no trials).

### F. Food, appetite and exercise

- Torres and Nowson 2007, Nutrition (abstract via PubMed API): stress shifts food intake in both directions, toward under-eating or over-eating, possibly depending on stressor severity. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nut.2007.08.008 . Strength: **weak to moderate** (older review). No study specific to appetite loss during infatuation was found.
- Ensari et al. 2015, Depression and Anxiety (abstract via PubMed API): 36 RCTs; one bout of exercise gives a small drop in state anxiety (g = 0.16) with high heterogeneity. https://doi.org/10.1002/da.22370 . Strength: **moderate** for a small effect.
- WHO physical activity fact sheet: adults need at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week; activity reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety and goes with better sleep. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity
- NHS insomnia page (C7): exercise regularly during the day, ending at least 4 hours before bedtime.
- Cross-link that matters: appetite loss plus drinking on a date plus insulin or sulphonylureas stacks the hypo risk (Diabetes UK: an empty stomach raises the risk, which lasts up to 24 hours).

### G. Crisis handling and stalking

**G1. Asking directly about suicide.**
- Samaritans: evidence shows asking someone if they are suicidal can protect them; always take it seriously; you do not have to solve their problems; in immediate danger call an ambulance (999 in the UK); Samaritans 116 123, free, day or night. https://www.samaritans.org/how-we-can-help/if-youre-worried-about-someone-else/supporting-someone-suicidal-thoughts/ . Strength: **moderate** (expert charity guidance).
- Dazzi et al. 2014, Psychological Medicine (abstract via PubMed API; PubMed tags it as an editorial or commentary on the evidence): none of the reviewed studies found a significant rise in suicidal ideation from asking about it. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291714001299 . Strength: **moderate at most**.

**G2. Guidance for AI chatbots.** Open letter by suicide prevention clinicians and researchers (signatories include Ursula Whiteside, David Jobes, Christine Moutier, Jo Robinson and Thomas Insel), last updated 17 September 2025. https://nowmattersnow.org/open-letter-on-ai-safety/
- Say plainly "I am a machine" and point to people the person trusts.
- Never discourage disclosure to others; never encourage ending life, metaphors such as "going home" included.
- Never advise on methods; block that path every time; support reduced access to lethal means.
- Connect to crisis lines (988 in the US); support safety planning.
- Strength: **moderate** (expert consensus), the closest thing to AI-specific guidance found.

**G3. Crisis lines.**
- Find A Helpline: a free directory of verified helplines in 175+ countries, run by ThroughLine, which lists IASP as a key partner. https://findahelpline.com/
- 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (US): call or text 988, chat at chat.988lifeline.org, 24/7. https://988lifeline.org/
- Samaritans (UK and Ireland): 116 123 (see G1).
- Strength: **strong** as factual contact information (official service pages).
- Unverified, snippet only (excluded): a RAND / Psychiatric Services chatbot study, the VERA-MH rubric, a Psychiatric Times commentary, an OpenAI weekly message figure.

**G4. Stalking.**
- CDC: stalking is a pattern of harassing or threatening tactics. Examples: following and watching; showing up at home or work; leaving strange items; unwanted calls, texts, email, social media or photo messages; GPS tracking; spying with cameras or software; unwanted cards, flowers or gifts. https://www.cdc.gov/intimate-partner-violence/about/about-stalking.html
- Suzy Lamplugh Trust: a pattern of fixated and obsessive behaviour which is repeated, persistent and intrusive and causes fear of violence or alarm and distress. https://www.suzylamplugh.org/what-is-stalking
- Unverified, snippet only (excluded): the "FOUR" acronym (fixated, obsessive, unwanted, repeated); it was absent from both pages opened.
- Strength: **moderate** (official public health definition plus NGO definition).
- Use in this skill runs in both directions: flag these behaviours when the crush or anyone else does them to the user, and also when the user drifts into them (repeated messages after silence, turning up uninvited, tracking).

## Part 2. Recommended changes to SKILL.md

Written as instructions an assistant can follow. Section numbers refer to the current SKILL.md.

### KEEP

1. **KEEP "say real risks clearly, once" (section 5, first bullet).** Finding: official sources (WHO, NHS, Diabetes UK, police) frame these as plain warnings; one clear statement informs the user and respects their choices.
2. **KEEP the redirect away from online digging (section 3).** Finding: CDC lists watching and contacting through social media and technology among stalking tactics, so this rule also keeps the user safely clear of that line.
3. **KEEP "leave while it is going well" and the planned ride home (section 4).** Finding: police guidance stresses your own transport and an easy exit (Virginia Tech Police, NZ Police).
4. **KEEP the professional referral when intensity eats sleep, food or work for days (Tone).** Finding: AASM 2021 recommends therapy for lasting insomnia and rates sleep tips alone as weak; NHS says see a GP when sleep trouble lasts or makes coping hard.

### CHANGE

5. **CHANGE the breathing instruction.** Offer the NHS slow breath as the default: "breathe in gently for a count of 5, out gently for a count of 5, for 5 minutes, ideally before you leave home". Offer cyclic sighing as a second option with the correct form: "one slow deep breath in through the nose, a short second sip of air on top, then a long slow breath out through the mouth, repeated for a few minutes". Present both as ways to settle the body that work best after a few days of practice. Finding: NHS breathing guidance; Balban 2023 tested 5 minutes daily for 28 days with a slow first inhale and found mood gains, while anxiety fell equally with mindfulness; Laborde 2022 shows slow breathing shifts heart rhythm during and right after one session.
6. **CHANGE "explain what is happening (adrenaline)" to include a medical safety line.** Explain the stress response in one sentence, then add once: "if you get chest pain, feel faint, or this feels unlike your usual nerves, get medical advice". Finding: the NHS panic page lists chest pain, faintness and a racing heart and says they can come from other conditions.
7. **CHANGE the sleep line (section 6) into a short kit for racing-mind nights.** When it is late or the user mentions lost sleep, offer one of these: write tomorrow's specific to-do list for 5 minutes before bed; park thoughts about the person in a 20 to 30 minute "worry time" earlier in the evening with pen and paper; put the phone away before bed; skip alcohol and coffee in the 6 hours before bed; keep the usual wake-up time. Finding: Scullin 2018 (d = 0.63, N = 57, one lab night); Oxford Health NHS worry time; NHS insomnia and Every Mind Matters advice.
8. **CHANGE "nights of lost sleep" into an honest framing.** Tell the user that people in early love often feel fine on short sleep, and that the cost still shows up as more anxiety and a shorter fuse the next day, which colours texts and decisions. Finding: Brand 2007 (in-love adolescents slept less and felt fine, self-report); Palmer 2024 (154 studies: sleep loss raises anxiety, SMD about 0.6); Ben Simon 2020.
9. **CHANGE the crisis bullet (section 5, third bullet) into a fuller protocol.** When the user mentions self-harm or suicide: step fully out of the game and the jokes; say plainly that you are an AI and want them connected with a person right now; ask directly whether they are thinking about ending their life; take any yes seriously; urge contact with local emergency services if they are in immediate danger; point to findahelpline.com for a free line in their country, with examples such as 988 (US, call or text) and Samaritans 116 123 (UK and Ireland); encourage them to tell someone they trust; keep talking with them. Keep all information about methods out of the conversation. Answer every mention of wanting to die, soft metaphors like "going home" included, on the side of staying alive and connected. Always support telling others. Finding: Samaritans guidance; Dazzi 2014 commentary; the clinicians' open letter on AI safety (2025); findahelpline.com; 988 Lifeline.

### ADD

10. **ADD a drink-driving rule that works in every country.** Say once: "drink-driving limits differ by country, some countries set zero, and new drivers often have lower limits; the simple rule is that whoever drives tonight drinks zero alcohol". Keep country numbers out of the skill text unless the user asks about their own country. Finding: WHO SAFER (0.05 g/dL general, 0.02 g/dL novice); WHO says crash risk starts at low BAC; EC and ETSC tables (0.0 in Czech Republic, Hungary, Romania and Slovakia; 0.8 in the UK except Scotland).
11. **ADD an alcohol and consent line for date plans.** When a plan includes drinks, say once: "alcohol makes it harder to read signals and hesitation, and a person who is very drunk cannot consent; keep it to a level where you both stay clear, and slow things down when either of you has been drinking". Finding: Abbey 2001 (alcohol raises misperception of interest and risk-taking); NHS Devon Sexual Health on consent and intoxication.
12. **ADD a spiking line.** If the user describes feeling far drunker than their drinks explain, or a gap in memory after a date, tell them to get to a safe place with someone they trust and seek urgent medical help, and mention that police and sexual assault services can help. Finding: NHS spiking page.
13. **ADD a diabetes and alcohol warning, general only.** When the user has diabetes and the plan includes drinks, say once: alcohol can cause low blood sugar for many hours afterwards, up to the next day, especially with insulin or some tablets; eating, carrying hypo treatment and medical ID, and telling the date or a friend how to help all lower the risk; a hypo can look like being drunk; and any change to medication or doses is a question for their own doctor or diabetes team. Keep dose numbers, carbohydrate amounts and medication adjustments out of every answer. Finding: Diabetes UK; American Diabetes Association.
14. **ADD the compounded-risk check.** When the user mentions little appetite or skipped meals, a date with drinks and diabetes medication in the same stretch, name the combination once and suggest a real meal before the date. Finding: Diabetes UK (an empty stomach and heavy drinking raise hypo risk, which lasts up to 24 hours); Torres and Nowson 2007 (stress can cut food intake).
15. **ADD a first-meeting safety checklist** for people met online or recently, offered once inside the date plan: meet in a busy public place; get there and back by your own transport; tell a friend where you are going, who you are meeting and when you expect to be home; keep your phone charged; keep your home address and workplace private until trust is earned; leave early if something feels wrong. Finding: NZ Police; Virginia Tech Police.
16. **ADD a money red flag.** If the person the user is falling for asks for money, gift cards, crypto or help with a visa or ticket, or keeps avoiding meeting in person, say once that these are the standard signs of a romance scam and suggest a reverse image search of their photos and a talk with someone they trust. Finding: US Federal Trade Commission.
17. **ADD a stalking flag that works in both directions.** If the user describes someone contacting them again and again after being ignored or told to stop, showing up at home or work, tracking them, or sending unwanted gifts, name it as the pattern safety services call stalking and point them to local police or a stalking helpline. If the user describes doing these things themselves (many messages after silence, turning up uninvited, watching someone's location), say once, kindly and clearly, that this frightens people and can count as stalking, then steer the energy back into the plan and their own life. Finding: CDC stalking tactics list; Suzy Lamplugh Trust definition.
18. **ADD an optional reappraisal line for pre-date nerves.** Offer once, as a light extra: "your racing heart is the same body state as excitement; try saying out loud 'I'm excited' before you walk in". Frame it as a way to feel more up for the moment, with no promise about how the date goes. Finding: Brooks 2014 (excitement up, anxiety unchanged); Poynter and Smith Pasqualini 2025 replication (same self-report result, no performance gain, N = 74); Jamieson line (moderate support in exams and lab tasks, mixed elsewhere).
19. **ADD a light movement suggestion.** On wired, restless days, suggest a walk or any activity the user enjoys during the day, finished at least 4 hours before bed. Finding: Ensari 2015 (small drop in state anxiety after one bout); WHO physical activity fact sheet; NHS insomnia timing.
20. **ADD a sleep red flag for people with bipolar disorder.** If the user mentions bipolar disorder, or several days of very little sleep with racing energy, say once that sleep loss is a known trigger of mood episodes for some people and suggest contacting their own clinician. Finding: Lewis 2017; Brand 2007 (early love itself scores high on a hypomania scale, so the two are easy to confuse).
21. **ADD a sleepy-driving line.** After nights of short sleep, include "if you are sleepy, someone else drives or you take a taxi" in the ride-home part of the plan. Finding: NHS insomnia page ("do not drive when you feel sleepy").

### REMOVE

22. **REMOVE the phrasing "two short inhales through the nose".** It misdescribes the studied technique, which starts with a slow full inhale and adds a short top-up (Balban 2023). Replace it with the wording in item 5.
23. **REMOVE any implication that one breath fixes nerves on the spot.** The trial evidence covers 5 minutes of daily practice; frame breathing as a few minutes before leaving home (Balban 2023; NHS breathing guidance).

## Part 3. Wording notes for the skill author

- Every health and safety line is said once per topic, in one or two plain sentences, then the conversation returns to the plan.
- For all medical questions (diabetes, medicines, chest symptoms, bipolar disorder), the skill sends the user to their own doctor or care team and gives general information only.
- Country-specific numbers (limits, hotline numbers) stay in examples; the default pointer is findahelpline.com plus "your local emergency number".
