# Research B: relationship initiation, first dates, social media checking

Scope: evidence behind the come-back-bro (intoxicated mode) skill, axis B.
Date of research: 2026-11-02.
Method: every finding below was read on a primary or near-primary page (PubMed record, publisher DOI page via OpenAlex metadata, author-hosted PDF, university repository PDF, pollster page). SAGE and ScienceDirect pages returned HTTP 403, so PubMed or author copies were used for those papers. Where only metadata could be opened, the finding is marked as such and no numbers are claimed.

Strength scale used:
- strong: several studies or a meta-analysis, large samples, live interaction, consistent results
- moderate: one solid multi-study paper or large sample, some limits
- weak: single small study, hypothetical scenario, old lab paradigm, or not replicated

## Part 1. Verified findings

### A. Self-disclosure

**A1. Disclosure and liking feed each other (meta-analysis).**
Collins and Miller 1994, Psychological Bulletin. Three effects all significant: people who make intimate disclosures tend to be liked more; people disclose more to those they already like; people come to like those they have disclosed to. Moderated by study paradigm, type of disclosure and gender of the discloser.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/ (abstract also via https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0033-2909.116.3.457)
Sample: meta-analysis; number of studies and effect sizes are absent from the abstract and were left unverified.
Strength: strong for the general link; the abstract gives no timing rule.

**A2. Gradual, escalating, back-and-forth disclosure creates closeness (the "36 questions").**
Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone and Bator 1997, PSPB 23(4), 363-377. Pairs spent 45 minutes on tasks where self-disclosure "gradually escalate[s] in intensity". Compared with small-talk tasks, this produced more closeness after the interaction (Study 1). Studies 2 and 3 found no significant closeness effects, despite adequate power, for matching pairs on attitudes, leading pairs to expect mutual liking, or making closeness an explicit goal.
Source: author-hosted copy of the abstract page https://stafforini.com/docs/Aron%20et%20al%20-%20The%20experimental%20generation%20of%20interpersonal%20closeness.pdf (SAGE page blocked). Sample sizes were unreadable in that copy.
Caveat: the procedure targets closeness between strangers (largely same-sex pairs in the lab) and makes no claim about creating romantic love.
Strength: moderate to strong (widely replicated procedure; romantic outcome untested here).

**A3. Taking turns matters: reciprocal disclosure beats one-way disclosure.**
Sprecher, Treger, Wondra, Hilaire and Wallpe 2013, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 49(5), 860-866. Pairs who alternated disclosing and listening reported more liking, closeness, perceived similarity and enjoyment than pairs where one person disclosed while the other only listened; the gap persisted after the one-way pairs swapped roles.
Source: metadata https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.017 ; abstract mirror https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/1961165.htm (ScienceDirect blocked).
Sample: unverified on a primary page (a figure of 156 appears only on student flashcard sites, so it is excluded).
Strength: moderate.

**A4. Early intimate disclosure was liked less than late disclosure (single old study).**
Wortman, Adesman, Herman and Greenberg 1976, JPSP 33(2), 184-191. A confederate made an intimate disclosure either early or near the end of a 10-minute conversation. The early discloser was rated with more negative traits and liked significantly less. Unexpectedly, the confederate who accepted responsibility for the disclosed event drew more negative reactions.
Source: https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0022-3514.33.2.184
Sample: unverified.
Strength: weak (one lab study, 1976, confederate design, 10-minute window). It is the only direct evidence found for a penalty on early intimate disclosure.

**A5. People expect deep talk with strangers to be more awkward than it turns out to be.**
Kardas, Kumar and Epley 2022, JPSP 122(3), 367-398. Across many experiments, conversations between strangers felt less awkward and created more connection and happiness than participants expected; people overestimated the awkwardness of deep conversations most, and felt more connected to deep conversation partners than shallow ones. The cause: people expected others to care less about their disclosures than others actually did.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34591541/
Sample: about a dozen experiments (1a to 7b); per-study N unverified.
Strength: moderate to strong. Caveat: stranger conversations in research settings, outside dating.

**A6. Others judge your vulnerability more kindly than you do ("beautiful mess effect").**
Bruk, Scholl and Bless 2018, JPSP 115(2), 192-205. Seven studies: people rated their own showing of vulnerability (confessing romantic feelings, revealing bodily imperfections, asking for help) more negatively than they rated the same act by others. A 2022 follow-up (PSPB) found self-compassion reduces this self-other gap.
Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30024240/ and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34292104/
Strength: moderate (mostly hypothetical scenarios plus one real-life study per abstract).

**A7. People underestimate how much they were liked after a structured disclosure task (replication of the liking gap).**
Sprecher 2021, Current Psychology. 71 dyads did a structured self-disclosure task over Skype; participants underestimated how much they were liked afterwards; pre-interaction expectations shaped post-interaction reactions.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33642839/
Strength: moderate.

**What this means for the "keep your insecurities to yourself early" reminder.**
The evidence supports pacing: disclosure that escalates step by step and goes back and forth (A2, A3) and a mild caution against a heavy disclosure in the first minutes (A4, weak). The evidence does not support holding back all vulnerability until trust is earned: intimate disclosure is generally liked (A1), deep talk works better than people fear (A5), and listeners judge vulnerability more kindly than the speaker does (A6). Best fit with the data: match the other person's depth and go one step deeper at a time; small honest imperfections are fine early; heavy material (old wounds, ex stories, strong self-criticism) waits until the sharing is mutual.

### B. Questions and responsiveness

**B1. Asking questions, especially follow-up questions, increases liking.**
Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson and Gino 2017, JPSP 113(3), 430-452. Study 1: 398 participants (199 dyads) in online chats; people told to ask many questions (nine) were liked more by partners than people told to ask few (four), Cohen's d about .35. Responsiveness (listening, understanding, validation, care) explained the effect. Study 3: speed-dating data from 110 men and women; people who asked more follow-up questions were more likely to get a yes for a second date. Follow-up questions specifically predicted liking; full-switch questions (changing topic) were associated with lower responsiveness. People did not predict that questions would raise liking.
Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28447835/ ; full text https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/novelty/2017-huang.pdf (sample numbers read from this PDF).
Integrity check: co-author Francesca Gino. The Many Co-Authors Project lists this paper as entry 45 with "Studies by Gino: 0" of 4 (https://www.manycoauthors.org/gino/papers). The speed-dating data in Study 3 came from the Stanford team (Jurafsky, Ranganath, McFarland), per the paper's acknowledgments.
Authors' own limitation (Limitations section of the PDF): the link may be curvilinear; asking too many questions without reciprocal self-disclosure likely lowers liking; third-party observers preferred the people who answered questions.
Strength: strong for follow-up questions; moderate for "more questions in general".

**B2. In the same speed-dating corpus, filler questions went with failed dates; shared stories went with "clicking".**
McFarland, Jurafsky and Rawlings 2013, American Journal of Sociology 118(6), 1596-1649. 947 four-minute heterosexual speed dates, 110 people (56 men, 54 women), Stanford graduate students. Overall question count was negatively associated with clicking; questions were used "to keep a lagging conversation going" and by people "who had nothing to say" (examples: "Where are you from?", "What major are you?"). Successful dates featured "long runs of high-energy, shared stories and few questions". Women reported connection when men showed sympathy and appreciation and when interruptions showed understanding (finishing or echoing her point).
Source: author-course copy of the JSTOR PDF https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf (text extracted and read: sample line 1810, questions discussion pages 1637-1638).
Caveat: heterosexual, one elite student population, four-minute dates, correlational.
Strength: moderate.

**Reconciling B1 and B2:** both papers analyze the same Stanford speed-dating corpus. Generic interview questions hurt; follow-up questions about what the person just said help; the strongest dates are a back-and-forth of stories. Practical rule: ask a follow-up about what they just said, then trade a short story of your own.

**B3. Responsiveness raises liking and closeness; its effect on sexual interest depends on the person.**
Birnbaum and Reis 2012, PSPB 38(7), 946-958 (three studies, strangers discussing a negative event): perceiving a partner as responsive went with more sexual interest, mainly among less avoidant people. Birnbaum, Ein-Dor, Reis and Segal 2014, PSPB 40(10), 1341-1353: responsiveness raised perceived attractiveness for men judging women, and the effect did not hold for women judging men in those studies. Birnbaum et al. 2016, JPSP 111(4), 530-546: in couples, responsiveness raised desire, more strongly in women.
Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22517110/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25062930/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27399250/
Strength: moderate. Write "responsiveness raises liking and closeness"; for attraction to a stranger the evidence is mixed by gender and attachment style.

**B4. Shared laughter goes with dating interest.**
Hall 2015, Evolutionary Psychology 13(3). Study 3: opposite-sex strangers (N = 102 as reported) talked for 10-12 minutes. Male humor production and female responsive laughter were both associated with the female's dating interest; both partners' dating interest was associated with simultaneous laughter.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37924189/
Strength: weak to moderate (one study of interacting strangers, heterosexual).

### C. Playing hard to get, uncertainty, selectivity

**C1. Playing hard to get raises wanting and lowers liking, and only when the other person is already committed.**
Dai, Dong and Jia 2014, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 143(2), 521-526 (two studies). If A is psychologically committed to B, B playing hard to get increases A's wanting and decreases A's liking; if A is uncommitted, it decreases both.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23668234/
Strength: moderate. Note: published in JEP:General.

**C2. Uncertainty about a stranger's interest increased attraction in one small study.**
Whitchurch, Wilson and Gilbert 2011, Psychological Science 22(2), 172-175. Women viewing Facebook profiles of men were most attracted when unsure whether the men liked them a lot or an average amount; they thought about those men the most.
Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21169522/ ; sample of 47 female undergraduates at the University of Virginia per https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/02/110207142623.htm
Caveat: no live interaction, small single-sex sample. A replication thesis listed at Western Carolina University reportedly found no effect (seen only in a search snippet, unopened, so treat as unverified).
Strength: weak.

**C3. Uncertainty about romantic interest lowered desire in live online encounters.**
Birnbaum, Kanat-Maymon, Mizrahi, Barniv, Nagar, Govinden and Reis 2018, Computers in Human Behavior 85, 372-384. Six studies. Study 1: 51 women and 50 men, single and heterosexual; partners whose interest was certain were rated more sexually desirable (mean 3.15) than partners whose interest was uncertain (2.73). Explicit expressions of romantic interest increased certainty and appeal. Harry Reis called playing hard to get "folk wisdom at best" and "on life support".
Source: University of Rochester news release https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/relationships-uncertainty-are-you-really-in-to-me-323512/ (journal page unopened).
Strength: moderate.

**C4. Selective interest gets returned; interest in everyone backfires.**
Eastwick, Finkel, Mochon and Ariely 2007, Psychological Science 18(4), 317-319. 156 undergraduates in seven speed-dating sessions, 4-minute dates with 9 to 13 partners. If someone uniquely desired a particular partner, that partner tended to reciprocate (r = .14) and felt more chemistry (r = .20). People who desired everyone were desired less (r = -.41) and produced less chemistry (r = -.32), partly via seeming unselective. Similar pattern in a separate sample of 608 professional speed-daters.
Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17470256/ ; author PDF https://elifinkel.squarespace.com/s/Eastwicketal_2007_000.pdf
Strength: moderate.

**Net for the skill:** coldness and games lack support; a clear, specific signal of interest in this one person is the evidence-backed move (C3, C4), and "hard to get" at best trades liking for wanting (C1).

### D. Speed dating, predictability, how relationships actually go

**D1. Compatibility cannot be predicted from traits before meeting.**
Joel, Eastwick and Finkel 2017, Psychological Science 28(10), 1478-1489. Two speed-dating studies; over 100 self-report trait and preference measures per person. Machine learning predicted 4-18% of how much a person desires others in general and 7-27% of how desired they are in general, and predicted zero of the unique desire between two specific people.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28853645/
Strength: strong for its question.

**D2. In ongoing couples, what predicts relationship quality is the relationship itself.**
Joel et al. 2020, PNAS 117(32), 19061-19071. 43 longitudinal couple datasets from 29 labs. Top relationship-specific predictors: perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction, conflict. Individual differences added nothing beyond a person's own relationship-specific experiences. Change in relationship quality over time was largely unpredictable.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32719123/
Caveat: established couples, outside the first-weeks stage.
Strength: strong.

**D3. Familiarity helps attraction once people actually interact.**
Reis, Maniaci, Caprariello, Eastwick and Finkel 2011 (JPSP 101) found familiarity increased attraction in live interaction; the later adversarial collaboration (Finkel, Norton, Reis, Ariely et al. 2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(1), 3-19) concluded the effect depends on stage: awareness, surface contact, mutuality.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910379/ (the 2011 paper was seen only in search results, its PDF unopened).
Strength: moderate.

**D4. People lean toward starting and keeping relationships ("progression bias").**
Joel and MacDonald 2021, Personality and Social Psychology Review 25(4), 317-343 (review). People are biased toward pro-relationship decisions in initiation, investment and breakup.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34247524/
Strength: moderate (review and theory).

### E. How much to talk, when to leave, and post-date anxiety

**E1. People are liked more after a conversation than they think (the liking gap).**
Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom and Clark 2018, Psychological Science 29(11), 1742-1756. Five studies: 36 community members (Study 1a); 84 Yale students with free conversation (Study 2); 102 people with conversations lasting as long as they liked (Study 3); 118 people at "How to Talk to Strangers" workshops (Study 4); 102 first-year suitemates followed over a year (Study 5). People consistently underestimated how much partners liked them and enjoyed their company; partners did send liking signals, and people were too focused on their own self-critical thoughts to notice them. Shyer people showed a bigger gap. The gap lasted months and vanished at the last time point in Study 5.
Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30183512/ ; open PDF https://repository.essex.ac.uk/23025/1/The%20Liking%20Gap%20in%20Conversations.pdf
Boundary: the studies measure liking and enjoyment between strangers or roommates; romantic interest was outside their measures. Replicated in a disclosure setting by Sprecher 2021 (A7).
Strength: strong for liking; extrapolation to romantic interest is an inference.

**E2. The opposite bias shows up in unrequited love.**
Baumeister, Wotman and Stillwell 1993, JPSP 64(3), 377-394. Narrative accounts: would-be lovers believed the attraction had been mutual, that they had been led on, and that the rejection was never communicated definitely. Rejectors described themselves as morally innocent yet guilty, and many described the would-be lover's persistence as intrusive and annoying.
Source: https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0022-3514.64.3.377
Strength: moderate (retrospective narratives).
**Use of E1 plus E2 together:** the liking gap is a good answer to "I came across badly" after a date. It gives zero support to "they feel the same as me". Fact-grounding still applies to the second thought.

**E3. Talking more than half the time was liked more, within 30-70%.**
Hirschi, Wilson and Gilbert 2022, PSPB (DOI 10.1177/01461672221104927). Three studies. People believed they would be more likable speaking less than half the time ("reticence bias"). In Study 3, people randomly assigned to speak 30, 40, 50, 60 or 70% of a conversation with a stranger were more likable the more they spoke.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35818304/
Sample sizes: unverified (the author PDF at https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/ was image-only).
Caveat: stranger conversations, talk share capped at 70%.
Strength: moderate.

**E4. Conversations almost never end when both people want them to.**
Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney and Wilson 2021, PNAS 118(10). 932 conversations in two studies. Study 2 (strangers in the lab, 252 participants analyzed): conversations averaged 20.19 minutes; both people wanted to end at the same moment in 1.59% of conversations; in 46.83% both wanted it to end earlier; in 9.52% both wanted it to go longer. People's guesses about when the partner wanted to stop were off by a mean of 9.38 minutes.
Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33649209/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7958231/
Strength: moderate to strong. Implications: (a) ending a little early carries low risk; (b) the moment a date ended tells you almost nothing about how the other person felt, so it is a poor basis for a verdict.

**E5. Quick turn-taking goes with feeling connected (in-conversation only).**
Templeton, Chang, Reynolds, Cone LeBeaumont and Wheatley 2022, PNAS. 66 students in 322 conversations, 22 friends, 450 online listeners: faster response gaps predicted connection and enjoyment; slowing gaps artificially lowered ratings.
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794835
Note: these are millisecond gaps inside spoken conversation and say nothing about text reply speed. Listed only to block misuse.

### F. Asking clearly, taking a no, recovering

**F1. Rejecting someone face to face is harder than people imagine.**
Joel, Teper and MacDonald 2014, Psychological Science 25(12), 2233-2240. Two studies: singles were significantly less willing to reject unattractive or incompatible potential dates when they believed the situation was real than when it was hypothetical, partly out of concern for the other person's feelings.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25344347/
Strength: moderate. Implications: a polite yes to a coffee can carry kindness more than interest; a soft or delayed non-answer after a clear question is often the kind way people say no (inference).

**F2. Ambiguous rejection prolongs the pain on both sides; persistence reads as intrusive.**
Baumeister et al. 1993 (E2).
Strength: moderate.

**F3. Fate thinking goes with accepting ghosting.**
Freedman, Powell, Le and Williams 2019, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 36(3), 905-924. Study 1 (N = 554): stronger destiny beliefs ("meant to be or not") went with more favorable attitudes toward ghosting, more intention to ghost and more past ghosting; growth beliefs showed the opposite. Study 2 (N = 747) replicated the romantic findings.
Source: https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1177/0265407517748791
Strength: moderate. Relevance: "we are meant to be" talk is a destiny belief; grounding it is consistent with the data.

**F4. Treating a rejection as a verdict on who you are makes it linger.**
Howe and Dweck 2016, PSPB 42(1), 54-71. Five studies: people who believe personality is fixed change their self-definition after rejection, and this makes them fear future rejection and keeps the negative feeling alive.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26498977/
Strength: moderate.

**F5. Self-compassion predicts faster emotional recovery after a breakup.**
Sbarra, Smith and Mehl 2012, Psychological Science 23(3), 261-269. 109 divorcing adults; judge-rated self-compassion predicted less emotional intrusion, up to 9 months later.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22282874/
Caveat: divorce, far from a first-weeks crush; the authors ask for replication. Small Iranian trials on compassion therapy after "love failure" were seen only in search snippets and are excluded.
Strength: moderate for its population, weak as applied to crush rejection.

### G. After the date: texting

**G1. Texting the next morning scored best in a scenario study.**
Teichmann, Petrowsky, Boecker, Soliman and Loschelder 2026, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 43(2), 570-593, DOI 10.1177/02654075251377184. Pre-study N = 100; main experiment N = 543. Conditions: text immediately after the date, the next morning, or after two days. Next morning produced the highest relationship intentions (inverted U). Immediate texts were linked to perceived neediness; perceived reciprocity and reliability carried the effect. Women were more sensitive to timing.
Source: https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/how-the-timing-of-texting-triggers-romantic-interest-after-the-fi/
Caveat: participants imagined a fictional date (vignette), heterosexual sample. Group means appeared only in press coverage and are excluded.
Strength: weak to moderate. This is the only peer-reviewed texting study found; everything else on texting rules was pop media.

### H. Social media checking

**H1. More Facebook use predicted more Facebook-related jealousy, with a proposed feedback loop.**
Muise, Christofides and Desmarais 2009, CyberPsychology and Behavior 12(4), 441-444. 308 undergraduates; controlling for personal and relationship factors, Facebook use predicted Facebook jealousy. The authors propose a loop: ambiguous information triggers more checking, which exposes more ambiguous information.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19366318/
Strength: moderate (cross-sectional; loop is the authors' interpretation).

**H2. Anxious attachment predicts partner surveillance.**
Fox and Warber 2014, Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 17(1), 3-7. 328 college students: preoccupied and fearful attachment reported the highest interpersonal electronic surveillance; relational uncertainty did not predict surveillance.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23952623/
Marshall, Bejanyan, Di Castro and Lee 2013, Personal Relationships 20(1), 1-22: across two studies attachment anxiety went with more Facebook jealousy and surveillance; a one-week diary showed anxiety predicted surveillance, partly via daily jealousy.
Source: https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/6476 (sample size unverified).
Strength: moderate.

**H3. Checking an ex's profile goes with more distress and longing and less growth.**
Marshall 2012, Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 15(10), 521-526. 464 participants: Facebook surveillance of an ex was associated with more distress, negative feelings, sexual desire and longing for the ex, and lower personal growth, after controlling for offline contact and personality.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22946958/
Strength: moderate (cross-sectional).

**H4. Distress drives monitoring after a breakup.**
Fox and Tokunaga 2015, Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 18(9), 491-498. N = 431: post-breakup distress predicted monitoring the ex, especially for those who did not initiate the breakup.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26348808/
Strength: moderate.

**H5. Tokunaga 2011 (origin of the term "interpersonal electronic surveillance" in romance).**
Computers in Human Behavior 27(2), 705-713, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2010.08.014. Only the metadata could be opened (https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.chb.2010.08.014); the abstract was unavailable, so no findings are claimed from it.

**H6. How common pre-date lookups are.**
- YouGov UK, 31 August 2018 (Omnibus; sample size not stated): among 18-24 year olds, 53% always and 16% often look up dates on social media before a first date; 32% always and 19% often among 25-34. Among people who have looked someone up, 27% would be suspicious of a date with no social media (34% of women, 19% of men).
  Source: https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/21474-would-you-go-date-someone-who-didnt-have-digital-f
- YouGov US, fieldwork 27-28 May 2021, 1,260 adults including 462 who had used dating apps: 44% of app users had searched a date's social media profile before meeting (51% of women, 38% of men), 40% searched the name, 13% did a reverse-image search, 9% ran a background check (13% of women, 6% of men); 31% did none of these.
  Source: https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/36301-dating-apps-background-checks-safety-poll
- Note: the brief cited a "YouGov 2018" stat; the 2018 YouGov data found is British. A US 2018 YouGov figure could not be found.
Reading: a quick pre-date lookup is common and, especially for women meeting app strangers, partly a safety check.

**Overall caveat for section H:** every surveillance study above looked at current partners or exes, mostly college students, mostly cross-sectional. None studied a new crush in the first weeks. Applying these findings to a crush is an inference, a reasonable one given the shared mechanisms (anxious attachment, ambiguous information, rumination). The strongest direct reason to learn the person face to face comes from D1: pre-meeting information predicted zero of the unique attraction between two people.

### I. Searched for and found nothing primary

- The 20-second green / 40-second red "traffic light" for talking: no peer-reviewed source found; it circulates as a pop communication tip.
- A study on asking a date whether they are single: none found.
- A study on "three facts, one lie" or similar games: none found. Shared laughter (B4) is the closest evidence.
- "Keep half a story for next time" (an unfinished-story or cliffhanger effect on dates): none found.
- Studies of self-compassion after rejection by a crush (as opposed to divorce or breakup): only small trials seen in snippets, excluded.

## Part 3. Recommended changes to SKILL.md

Each item: one instruction an assistant can follow, then the finding behind it.

### KEEP

1. **KEEP the fact grounding against "we are meant to be".** Answer fate talk with the facts on the list and the next concrete step. Behind it: destiny beliefs go with ghosting attitudes (F3); people lean toward progression even with poor fits (D4); would-be lovers tend to believe interest was mutual (E2).
2. **KEEP "close the profile, you learn them up close".** Turn each "I want to know X" into a question for the next meeting. Behind it: 100+ pre-date measures predicted zero of the unique attraction between two people (D1); surveillance tracks anxious attachment, jealousy and distress (H1 to H4, partners and exes, inferred for crushes).
3. **KEEP tossing coldness and manipulation tactics from internet advice.** Behind it: playing hard to get lowers liking and only raises wanting in already-committed targets (C1); uncertainty about interest lowered desire in live encounters (C3); selective, clear interest gets returned (C4). The supportive study (C2) is small, uses profiles only and has a reported null replication.
4. **KEEP the light games, capped at two.** Present them as ways to laugh together. Behind it: shared laughter and responsive laughter go with dating interest (B4).
5. **KEEP "leave while it is going well".** Behind it: in lab conversations between strangers, both people wanted it to end earlier in 46.83% of cases and both wanted more in 9.52% (E4).
6. **KEEP "one short warm message, then give it room".** Behind it: G1 (timing), E2 (persistence feels intrusive).

### CHANGE

7. **CHANGE the 20/40 traffic light into the "follow-up, then a story" rhythm.** Tell the user: ask one follow-up question about what they just said, listen, then share a short story of your own that connects to it; if you notice a monologue, hand the ball back with a follow-up. Mention the 20/40 numbers only as an optional personal rule of thumb for chronic over-talkers. Behind it: follow-up questions predicted second-date yeses (B1); filler questions went with failed dates and shared stories with clicking (B2); turn-taking disclosure beats one-way disclosure (A3); talking more was liked more within 30-70% (E3); no primary source exists for 20/40 (I).
8. **CHANGE the example reminder about insecurities to a pacing rule.** Suggested wording: "Match their depth and go one small step deeper at a time. Small honest imperfections are fine early. Heavy things (old wounds, ex stories, harsh self-criticism) wait until the sharing is mutual." The skill keeps honoring whatever rule the user actually sets. Behind it: escalating reciprocal disclosure builds closeness (A2, A3); intimate disclosure is generally liked (A1); deep talk goes better than expected (A5); others judge vulnerability more kindly than the speaker (A6); the only direct penalty for early intimate disclosure is one 1976 lab study (A4, weak).
9. **CHANGE "Next day" to "next morning".** Behind it: next morning beat both immediate and two days later in the only experiment found (G1, vignette, N = 543). Say it is one study.
10. **CHANGE the drink bet to a stake that is a small treat or a choice** (dessert, the next song, who picks the next place). Behind it: no study on the game; the skill's own safety rule on alcohol.
11. **CHANGE the single-status question into a clarity principle.** Tell the user: it is fine to ask plainly and kindly whether they are seeing someone; a clear answer is a kindness to both people; if they are taken, the plan ends there. Behind it: uncertainty lowers desire and wastes hope (C3, E2). No study tests the question itself.

### ADD

12. **ADD the liking gap as the standard answer to post-date self-criticism, with its boundary.** When the user replays the date and says "I came across badly", tell them: research shows people are liked more after a conversation than they think, and shyer people underestimate most; then point to the real signals they reported. Keep the facts list in charge for "they feel the same as me": the liking gap covers liking and enjoyment, and would-be lovers tend to overestimate romantic mutuality. Behind it: E1 (five studies), A7 (replication), E2.
13. **ADD a short "how to ask clearly and how to take a no" section.** Tell the user: make one clear, specific invitation that names the plan; accept the first clear no warmly and stop; treat a vague or very delayed reply to a clear ask as a soft no and wait for them to come forward; one message, then silence until they answer. Behind it: rejecting someone face to face is hard, so people soften and delay (F1); ambiguous rejection prolongs pain, and persistence reads as intrusive (E2).
14. **ADD rejection recovery framed as fit.** When a no comes, tell the user: this is information about fit between two people, and it leaves who you are unchanged; then use warm, plain self-talk and a concrete next commitment. Behind it: tying rejection to self-definition makes it linger (F4); self-compassion predicted recovery after divorce (F5, weaker for crushes); attraction between two specific people is unpredictable in advance (D1).
15. **ADD "the ending of the date tells you nothing".** When the user worries that the date ended too soon or too late, tell them: research shows people cannot tell when the other person wanted to stop, and conversations almost never end at the moment both want. Behind it: E4.
16. **ADD a safety exception to the no-digging rule.** Before meeting a stranger from an app, one quick check that the person is real (name and photos match, nothing alarming in public) is a safety step; it happens once before the first meeting and stops there. Behind it: 44% of US app users search a date's profile and 13% reverse-image search, women more often (H6). This exception is a safety judgment; no study tests it.
17. **ADD a self-check for anxious checking.** If the user keeps checking the person's online activity, name it once as the anxiety loop (ambiguous post, worry, more checking) and redirect to the next real contact or a task. Behind it: H1 (feedback loop), H2 (anxious attachment predicts surveillance), H4 (distress predicts monitoring).
18. **ADD "show specific interest in this person".** In plans and messages, help the user show interest that is clearly about this person (a detail they mentioned, a callback to a joke). Behind it: unique desire gets reciprocated and generalized eagerness gets the opposite (C4); clear interest raises appeal (C3); responsiveness raises liking (B1, B3).
19. **ADD follow-up questions to the plan builder.** When building "the questions" for a date, prepare two or three openers plus the habit of following up; skip interview lists of facts (job, hometown, siblings). Behind it: B1, B2.

### REMOVE

20. **REMOVE the specific 20/40 second numbers as a rule** (keep the idea of passing the ball back, item 7). Behind it: no primary source (I); E3 points the other way on total talk share.
21. **REMOVE "keep half a story for next time" as a stated tactic,** or keep it as a playful habit with no claim attached. Behind it: no evidence found (I).

## Part 4. Caveats on this evidence base

- Most first-impression research uses heterosexual US college students and short lab or speed-dating encounters (B1, B2, C2, C4, D1, E1). Effects in other cultures, ages and in queer dating are largely untested here.
- Disclosure and liking-gap studies mostly involve strangers or roommates, outside romance; their transfer to dates is reasonable and still an inference.
- The texting evidence is one vignette experiment (G1).
- Social media surveillance evidence concerns partners and exes and is mostly cross-sectional (H1 to H4).
- Numbers that appeared only in search snippets or press coverage were excluded: Montoya et al. 2018 figures, the Sprecher 2013 sample, the Marshall 2013 sample, Teichmann group means, and a "60%" figure from a dating-industry site.
- Huang et al. 2017 has Francesca Gino as a co-author; the Many Co-Authors Project lists zero of its four studies as run by Gino, and its speed-dating data came from the Stanford team.
